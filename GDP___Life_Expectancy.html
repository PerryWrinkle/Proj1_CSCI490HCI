Prosperity and the length of a life

Economists have long noticed that as a country's income rises, so does the length of the lives its citizens can expect to live — but not forever, and not evenly. Plotted together, GDP per person and life expectancy trace a curve that climbs steeply among poor countries, bends hard through the middle-income range, and nearly flattens out among the rich. This shape, first described by the demographer Samuel Preston in the 1970s, tells a story that a simple "richer is healthier" headline misses: money buys health mostly through what it is spent on — clean water, vaccines, maternal care, trauma surgery, pollution control — and once those basics are secured, additional income buys comparatively little extra lifespan. The twenty countries gathered in the table beside this text, spanning Norway's oil wealth to Nigeria's young and fast-growing population, illustrate almost every part of that curve at once.

At the very top of the income scale, the returns are already thin. Switzerland's GDP per capita of roughly $99,000 is the highest in this set, and its citizens live to about 84.0 years on average — genuinely excellent, but not dramatically better than several countries earning half as much. Norway, at close to $87,900 per person, records a life expectancy of 83.2 years, while the United States, with a GDP per capita near $81,000 and an economy growing at 2.5% a year, actually trails both of them at just 79.3 years. That gap — the richest large economy in the world posting a shorter average lifespan than several less wealthy peers — is one of the clearest signals that GDP alone is not destiny. It points instead to how a country's wealth is distributed and spent: on insurance coverage, on chronic disease prevention, on the social safety net that catches people before illness becomes catastrophe.

The point becomes even sharper further down the income ladder. Japan earns only about $33,800 per capita — a little over a third of Switzerland's figure — yet its people live longest of anyone in this table, to an average of 84.5 years. South Korea tells much the same story at $33,100 per capita and a life expectancy of 83.5 years, essentially matching countries with nearly triple its income. Australia, meanwhile, reaches 83.3 years on a considerably higher income base, and the Netherlands, earning around $61,800 per person, sits in the same cluster. Diet, walkable cities, low rates of gun violence, and above all universal, low-friction access to a doctor appear to matter as much as the size of the paycheck once a country clears a fairly modest income threshold.

Western Europe as a bloc demonstrates that same convergence across a surprisingly wide range of incomes. Germany, at about $54,300 per capita, reports a life expectancy of 81.0 years; the United Kingdom, earning less at roughly $49,900, reaches a slightly higher 81.3 years; France climbs to 82.5 years; and Italy and Spain, both around 83.1 and 83.3 years respectively, post some of the best outcomes in the entire dataset despite middling GDP figures by rich-world standards. Canada, at close to $53,400 per capita, lands at 81.7 years. What these economies share is not simply wealth but universal health coverage funded through taxation, which decouples a person's chance of surviving an illness from their individual income — a structural choice, not an automatic consequence of GDP.

Move into the large middle-income economies and the picture becomes more textured. China, with a GDP per capita of about $12,600, an economy still expanding at 5.2% annually, and a population of 1,412 million, has pushed life expectancy up to 78.6 years — within striking distance of the United States despite a fraction of its income. Brazil, at roughly $10,400 per capita, reaches 75.5 years, and Mexico, similar in income at about $13,800, reaches 75.0. Russia is the outlier of this group: at around $14,400 per capita — higher than Brazil or China — its life expectancy is only 72.7 years, the lowest among the middle-income economies here. Elevated rates of cardiovascular disease, alcohol-related mortality, and a health system under strain pull its outcomes below what its income alone would predict, a reminder that the Preston curve describes a tendency, not a law.

At the lower end of the income spectrum, the gap between wealth and health widens again, and the stakes of that gap are measured in years of life. Indonesia, growing at 5.0% annually from a base of about $4,900 per capita, has reached 71.9 years. India, the fastest-growing major economy in this table at 7.0% a year but starting from a GDP per capita of only about $2,600 and a population of 1,428 million, has nonetheless lifted life expectancy to 70.9 years — proof that rapid growth, sustained over decades, does eventually translate into longer lives, even if the starting point was low. Nigeria sits furthest down the curve: a GDP per capita of roughly $1,700 and modest growth of 2.9% coincide with a life expectancy of just 55.0 years, nearly three decades short of Japan's figure. That gap is driven less by any single disease than by the accumulated weight of unsafe water, limited maternal care, infectious disease, and a young health system trying to serve one of the fastest-growing populations on earth.

Taken together, the twenty rows in this table describe an arc rather than a straight line. Among poor and lower-middle-income countries, every additional dollar of GDP tends to buy a real and visible improvement in how long people live, because it funds the basics that keep infants, mothers, and the very sick alive. Among wealthy countries, that same dollar buys much less, and the differences that remain — Japan and South Korea outliving the United States, Italy and Spain outliving Germany, Russia trailing its income peers — are explained by how healthcare is organized and funded rather than by the size of the economy. The practical lesson for policymakers is not that growth is unimportant, but that growth is a means rather than an end: a government that wants its citizens to live longer needs an explicit plan for turning national income into accessible medical care, safe food and water, and a social safety net, because GDP on its own has no mechanism for delivering any of it.
